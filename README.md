# Climate-Change1
We analyzed the Our World in Data CO2 dataset to evaluate how per-capita emissions compare across nations of varying economic wealth (GDP per capita) and identified high-income countries that successfully decouple economic growth from heavy pollution.
High individual carbon footprints accelerate global climate change, yet policy discussions often focus strictly on total national emissions rather than per-capita impacts. Without a clear understanding of individual footprint patterns, lower-income communities face disproportionate environmental harms while climate funding and regulations risk being misallocated. Evaluating per-capita emissions empowers local policymakers to focus on transitioning energy grids to renewables rather than targeting overall population growth.
We used the 2024 Our world In Data CO2 and Greenhouse Gas Emissions dataset focusing on key variables like co2 per capita, gdp per capita, isocode and year. After cleaning the data set- specifically dropping non-country regional aggregates where iso_code.isnull()—we analyzed nation-level records from 2000 to 2021. During EDA, we were surprised to find extreme variance among equally wealthy nations: high-income oil producers like Qatar emitted over 35 metric tonnes per person, while equally wealthy nations like Switzerland emitted under 4 metric tonnes per person thanks to clean power grids.
We aimed to determine how per-capita CO2 emissions correlate with economic wealth (GDP per capita) and track how top emitters shifted over time. Using Pandas, we cleaned aggregate noise and defined a success benchmark (GDP per capita > $$30,000 paired with emissions $5 metric tonnes per person). We evaluated our findings through Seaborn visualizations—including distribution histograms, bar charts, line graphs, and scatter plots—to examine right-skewness, track historical changes (2000–2021), and isolate low-emission, high-income outliers.
Our primary result shows that while the top 10 emitting nations produced over 25 billion metric tonnes of C02 in 2024, the vast majority of individual countries emit under 5 metric tonnes per person annually. The scatter plot comparing (GDP per capita) against co2_per_capita best tells the story, clearly illustrating the long right-tail skew while highlighting clean-energy exceptions like Switzerland that achieve high economic output without high per-person emissions.
We evaluated representation equity across geographic regions, economic brackets, and time periods. Major developed economies were heavily represented with complete recent records, whereas smaller developing nations and historical eras prior to recent decades were underrepresented or invisible due to missing data. Filling missing values with zero would have unfairly undercounted developing nations; recognizing this bias ensured we evaluated climate responsibility through per-capita equity rather than incorrectly placing sole blame on populous or developing countries.
A major limitation was incomplete historical reporting for lower-income countries and the reliance on production-based emissions rather than consumption-based emissions. With another week, we would incorporate consumption-based carbon data to verify whether low-emitting wealthy nations genuinely run clean grids or simply outsource their carbon-heavy manufacturing overseas. Readers should not claim based on these results that economic growth strictly dictates high pollution, nor should they use total national totals alone to assess an individual citizen's footprint.
